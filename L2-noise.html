In the previous chapter, we described in words what can go wrong in a quantum device, its noise (Section 1.5). In this chapter, we describe noise mathematically. The goal is to model how noise affects the results of a computation, and to use these models in our estimates and simulations. One model runs through the whole chapter: a noisy operation is the ideal operation followed by a quantum channel, a map that turns the state of the qubits into a noisier one.

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to write a channel with Kraus operators and work out what it does to Pauli expectation values; relate \(T_1\), \(T_2^*\) and \(T_2\) to the channels behind them; tell local from global noise, and coherent from incoherent errors; model readout errors; estimate how much signal a circuit keeps; and separate the bias caused by noise from shot noise.

2.1 Density matrices and channels

We first review the two tools that the rest of the chapter uses throughout. If you know them well, skim this section for the notation.

2.1.1 Mixed states

Noise makes the state of a device uncertain. If the qubits are in the state \(\ket{\psi_k}\) with probability \(p_k\), the expectation value of an observable \(O\) is \[ \sum_k p_k \bra{\psi_k}O\ket{\psi_k} = \Tr[O\rho], \qquad \text{with} \quad \rho = \sum_k p_k \ket{\psi_k}\bra{\psi_k} . \] The probability of each measurement outcome is the expectation value of a projector, so all predictions depend on the ensemble only through \(\rho\).

Definition 2.1 (Density matrix) A density matrix on \(n\) qubits is a \(2^n\times 2^n\) matrix \(\rho\) that is positive semidefinite (Hermitian, with no negative eigenvalues) and has trace one. The expectation value of an observable \(O\) is \(\langle O\rangle = \Tr[O\rho]\). A pure state \(\ket{\psi}\) has \(\rho = \ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\), and then \(\langle O\rangle = \bra{\psi}O\ket{\psi}\). Every other state is mixed.

A density matrix is pure exactly when \(\rho^2 = \rho\), or, equivalently, when \(\Tr[\rho^2] = 1\). The purity \(\Tr[\rho^2]\) measures how mixed a state is. The eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\) of \(\rho\) are non-negative and sum to one, and the purity is \(\sum_k\lambda_k^2\). It is 1 when one eigenvalue is 1 and the others are 0, which is a pure state. It is smallest, \(2^{-n}\), when all \(2^n\) eigenvalues are equal, which is the maximally mixed state \(I/2^n\): the qubits are in a uniformly random basis state, and we know nothing about them.

For a single qubit, we want to extend the Bloch-sphere picture of pure states (Figure 1.7) to mixed states. Every Hermitian \(2\times 2\) matrix can be written in the Pauli basis \(I, X, Y, Z\), with real coefficients. The Pauli matrices have trace zero, and the product of two different ones is the third, up to a factor (Section 1.3.1), so \(\Tr[PQ] = 2\) if \(P = Q\) and \(0\) otherwise. If we write \(\rho = \frac{1}{2}(c_I I + c_x X + c_y Y + c_z Z)\), the trace of \(P\rho\) therefore picks out one coefficient at a time: \(c_I = \Tr[\rho] = 1\) and \(c_x = \Tr[X\rho] = \langle X\rangle\), and likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). A density matrix of one qubit is therefore \[ \rho = \frac{1}{2}\left(I + r_x X + r_y Y + r_z Z\right), \qquad r_x = \langle X\rangle,\ r_y = \langle Y\rangle,\ r_z = \langle Z\rangle . \tag{2.1}\] The vector \(\vec r = (r_x, r_y, r_z)\) is the Bloch vector. The matrix \(r_x X + r_y Y + r_z Z\) squares to \(|\vec r|^2 I\), since the Pauli matrices anticommute. The eigenvalues of \(\rho\) are therefore \((1\pm|\vec r|)/2\), and \(\rho\) is a density matrix exactly when \(|\vec r| \le 1\). Pure states lie on the surface of the Bloch sphere, and mixed states inside it, in the Bloch ball. At the centre lies the maximally mixed state \(I/2\), a uniformly random bit. The purity is \(\Tr[\rho^2] = (1+|\vec r|^2)/2\): 1 on the sphere, and \(1/2\) at the centre.

The same decomposition works for any number of qubits, with Pauli strings in place of Pauli matrices: \[ \rho = \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{P} \langle P\rangle\, P , \tag{2.2}\] where the sum runs over all \(4^n\) Pauli strings on \(n\) qubits, and \(\langle I\rangle = 1\). To see why, note two facts. The product of two Pauli strings is, up to a factor, another Pauli string, which is the identity only if the two are equal. And every Pauli string other than the identity has trace zero. So \(\Tr[PQ] = 2^n\) if \(P = Q\) and \(0\) otherwise: the \(4^n\) Pauli strings are orthogonal, and they form a basis of all \(2^n\times 2^n\) matrices. Multiplying the right-hand side of Equation 2.2 by a Pauli string \(Q\) and taking the trace keeps only the term \(P = Q\), and gives \(\Tr[Q\rho] = \langle Q\rangle\), as it should. The expectation values of the Pauli strings fix the state, as the Bloch vector does for one qubit, and most of this chapter follows what noise does to them. To describe some of the qubits on their own, we discard the others with the partial trace, defined by \(\Tr_B[A\otimes B] = \Tr[B]\,A\). In Equation 2.2, it keeps the terms whose Pauli strings act as the identity on the discarded qubits.

2.1.2 Quantum channels

Noise turns one density matrix into another. The maps that describe this are called quantum channels.

Definition 2.2 (Quantum channel) A quantum channel maps a density matrix \(\rho\) to \[ \Lambda(\rho) = \sum_k K_k\, \rho\, K_k^\dagger , \qquad \text{with} \quad \sum_k K_k^\dagger K_k = I . \tag{2.3}\] The matrices \(K_k\) are its Kraus operators.

Each Kraus operator describes one thing that can happen to the qubit, with probability \(\Tr[K_k\rho K_k^\dagger]\). The condition on the Kraus operators keeps the trace, since \(\Tr[\Lambda(\rho)] = \Tr\big[\sum_k K_k^\dagger K_k\,\rho\big] = \Tr[\rho]\), so these probabilities sum to one. Each term \(K_k\rho K_k^\dagger\) is also positive semidefinite, so \(\Lambda(\rho)\) is again a density matrix. This still holds if the channel acts on only some of the qubits of a larger system, a property called complete positivity. It is stronger than positivity: the transpose, for example, is positive but not completely positive. Conversely, every linear map that is completely positive and keeps the trace (CPTP) has a Kraus form. More details, with proofs, can be found in Nielsen and Chuang (2010, sec. 8.2) and in the Quantum Information Theory notes of Walter and Ozols (2025, Theorem 5.3 and Lemma 5.5, where the Kraus operators are called \(X_i\)).

Where do channels come from? A qubit is never perfectly isolated (Section 1.2.1): it interacts with its environment, for example with defects in the chip (Section 1.5). Qubit and environment together evolve unitarily, but we do not keep track of the environment, so we discard it with the partial trace. The result is always a channel on the qubit. Conversely, every channel can arise in this way, and the unitary and the environment that produce it are called a Stinespring dilation of the channel. Exercise 2.10 works out an example, and more details can be found in Walter and Ozols (2025, sec. 5.2).

A unitary gate \(U\) is a channel with the single Kraus operator \(U\). Applying \(\Lambda_1\) and then \(\Lambda_2\) gives a channel with the Kraus operators \(K^{(2)}_j K^{(1)}_k\). And if \(\Lambda_i\) happens with probability \(p_i\), the mixture \(\sum_i p_i\Lambda_i(\rho)\) is a channel with the Kraus operators \(\sqrt{p_i}\,K^{(i)}_k\). Most noise in this chapter is of this last kind: with some probability, something goes wrong.

2.2 Noise on one qubit

2.2.1 Pauli channels on the Bloch sphere

The simplest errors are Pauli matrices: \(X\) flips the bit, \(Z\) flips the sign of the relative phase between \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\), and \(Y\) does both.

Definition 2.3 (Pauli channel) A Pauli channel on one qubit applies the error \(Q \in \{I, X, Y, Z\}\) with probability \(q_Q\): \[ \Lambda(\rho) = q_I\,\rho + q_X\, X\rho X + q_Y\, Y\rho Y + q_Z\, Z\rho Z, \qquad q_I + q_X + q_Y + q_Z = 1 . \] Its Kraus operators are \(K_Q = \sqrt{q_Q}\,Q\), for \(Q \in \{I, X, Y, Z\}\).

What does a Pauli channel do to the Bloch vector? Conjugating a Pauli matrix by another gives back the same matrix, up to a sign: \(QPQ = P\) if \(Q = I\) or \(Q = P\), and \(QPQ = -P\) otherwise, because different Pauli matrices anticommute. The channel therefore maps each Pauli matrix to a multiple of itself, \(\Lambda(X) = f_x X\), and likewise for \(Y\) and \(Z\), with \[ \begin{aligned} f_x &= q_I + q_X - q_Y - q_Z , \\ f_y &= q_I - q_X + q_Y - q_Z , \\ f_z &= q_I - q_X - q_Y + q_Z , \end{aligned} \tag{2.4}\] while \(\Lambda(I) = I\). By Equation 2.1, the channel multiplies each component of the Bloch vector by its own factor, \(r_j \mapsto f_j r_j\), and so each expectation value: \(\langle X\rangle \mapsto f_x\langle X\rangle\), and so on. We call \(f_x\), \(f_y\) and \(f_z\) the shrinking factors of the channel. They are the main tool of this chapter: as long as the noise is a Pauli channel, its effect on a measured expectation value is a multiplication. In the Bloch ball, a Pauli channel squeezes the ball into an ellipsoid with semi-axes \(|f_x|\), \(|f_y|\) and \(|f_z|\), centred at the maximally mixed state.

2.2.2 Depolarising and dephasing noise

Two Pauli channels describe most of the noise on a single qubit in this chapter.

Definition 2.4 (Depolarising and dephasing channels) The depolarising channel on one qubit is the Pauli channel with \(q_X = q_Y = q_Z = p/4\): \[ \Lambda(\rho) = \Big(1 - \frac{3p}{4}\Big)\rho + \frac{p}{4}\big(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z\big) . \] The dephasing channel is the Pauli channel with \(q_Z = p\): \[ \Lambda(\rho) = (1-p)\,\rho + p\, Z\rho Z . \]

The depolarising channel treats the three axes alike. By Equation 2.4, its factors are \(f_x = f_y = f_z = 1 - p\). It shrinks the Bloch ball uniformly towards its centre (Figure 2.1 a), and every expectation value with it: \(\langle P\rangle \mapsto (1-p)\langle P\rangle\). By Equation 2.1, the state with the Bloch vector \((1-p)\vec r\) is the mixture \((1-p)\rho + p\,I/2\), so \[ \Lambda(\rho) = (1-p)\,\rho + p\,\frac{I}{2} : \tag{2.5}\] with probability \(p\), the depolarising channel replaces the state by the maximally mixed state. This form gives other Kraus operators: \(\sqrt{1-p}\,I\) and the four matrices \(\sqrt{p/2}\,\ket{i}\bra{j}\) with \(i, j \in \{0, 1\}\). The same channel can therefore have different Kraus operators. The depolarising channel also commutes with every single-qubit unitary \(U\): \(U\Lambda(\rho)U^\dagger = (1-p)\,U\rho U^\dagger + p\,I/2 = \Lambda(U\rho U^\dagger)\), since \(U(I/2)U^\dagger = I/2\).

The dephasing channel has the factors \(f_x = f_y = 1 - 2p\) and \(f_z = 1\). It leaves \(\langle Z\rangle\), and with it the probabilities of measuring 0 and 1, unchanged. It shrinks the components of the Bloch vector in the \(xy\) plane, which carry the relative phase between \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\). The ball is squeezed towards the \(z\) axis (Figure 2.1 b), and for \(p = 1/2\) it collapses onto it. It is also called the phase-flip channel, after its Kraus form, a \(Z\) error with probability \(p\). The name suggests a phase that now and then flips suddenly, but dephasing usually comes from a gradual loss of information about the phase, as we see below.

The physical origin of dephasing. The relative phase of a superposition \(\alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1}\) turns at a rate set by the qubit’s frequency (Section 1.5), and the device is calibrated to expect this. Suppose that the frequency of the qubit is shifted by a small amount \(\delta\) (in radians per second), for example by a fluctuating magnetic field. The qubit then picks up an extra phase that nobody expected: in a time \(t\), its relative phase turns by an extra angle \(\theta = \delta t\). This is the rotation \(R_Z(\theta)\) of Equation 1.2. If we knew \(\delta\), we could undo this rotation. If we do not know it, we do not know \(\theta\) either, and the qubit undergoes a random rotation. Its state becomes the mixture \(\Lambda(\rho) = \mathbb{E}\big[R_Z(\theta)\,\rho\,R_Z(\theta)^\dagger\big]\), where \(\mathbb{E}\) is the average over \(\theta\). By Exercise 1.4, \(R_Z(\theta)XR_Z(\theta)^\dagger = \cos\theta\, X + \sin\theta\, Y\). For an angle that is as likely to be positive as negative, the sines average to zero: \[ \Lambda(X) = \mathbb{E}[\cos\theta]\, X, \qquad \Lambda(Y) = \mathbb{E}[\cos\theta]\, Y, \qquad \Lambda(Z) = Z . \] These are the factors of a dephasing channel with \(1 - 2p = \mathbb{E}[\cos\theta]\): an unknown phase acts as dephasing. The longer the qubit waits, the wider the spread of \(\theta = \delta t\), and the more information about the phase is lost. Once the spread is much larger than a radian, \(\mathbb{E}[\cos\theta]\) is close to zero and \(p\) close to \(1/2\): the phase is lost. For a Gaussian angle with variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\mathbb{E}[\cos\theta] = e^{-\sigma^2/2}\) (Exercise 2.1).

Exercise 2.1 (Random phases) A qubit undergoes the rotation \(R_Z(\theta)\), where \(\theta\) is a Gaussian random angle with mean zero and variance \(\sigma^2\).

  1. Show that \(\mathbb{E}[\cos\theta] = e^{-\sigma^2/2}\).

    Hint

    \(\cos\theta\) is the real part of \(e^{i\theta}\).

  2. Which dephasing probability \(p\) does this channel have? What is it for \(\sigma \ll 1\)?

2.2.3 Noise gate by gate

A common approximation of the noise on a device models each noisy gate as the ideal gate \(U\) followed by a channel \(\Lambda\), so that it maps \(\rho\) to \(\Lambda(U\rho U^\dagger)\). A circuit of noisy gates is then a composition of channels. Quantum software development kits (SDKs), such as the Qiskit of the tutorials, usually describe noise in this way. The approximation assumes that the noise has no memory: the channel after each gate does not depend on what happened before. Noise of this kind is called Markovian. Section 2.2.6 describes the main exception. The shrinking factors make compositions easy: two Pauli channels in a row multiply each expectation value by the factors of both.

Exercise 2.2 (Composing depolarising channels)  

  1. Apply the depolarising channel with parameter \(p_1\) and then the one with \(p_2\), using Equation 2.5. Show that the result is again a depolarising channel, and check that its factor is \((1-p_1)(1-p_2)\).

  2. A qubit is prepared in \(\ket{+}\), and \(n\) noisy \(X\) gates are applied, each followed by depolarising noise with parameter \(p\). What is \(\langle X\rangle\) at the end? For \(p = 10^{-3}\), after how many gates has \(\langle X\rangle\) fallen to half of its initial value?

    Hint

    Where in the circuit can the channels be moved?

  3. Two qubits each undergo depolarising noise with parameter \(p\), independently, so that the channel on both is \(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda\). By which factors do \(\langle Z\otimes I\rangle\) and \(\langle Z\otimes Z\rangle\) shrink? Do these factors depend on the state of the two qubits?

    Hint

    A product of channels acts factor by factor: \((\Lambda\otimes\Lambda)(A\otimes B) = \Lambda(A)\otimes\Lambda(B)\).

2.2.4 Relaxation and non-unital noise

The channels so far are Pauli channels, and they share a property: they map the maximally mixed state to itself, \(\Lambda(I/2) = I/2\), so the centre of the Bloch ball stays where it is. A channel with this property is called unital. Unital noise can only make a state more mixed: it destroys information, but it does not push the qubit towards any particular state.

Relaxation, the decay of \(\ket{1}\) to \(\ket{0}\) (Section 1.5), is different. A qubit in \(\ket{1}\) gives its energy to the environment and ends up in \(\ket{0}\). Relaxation therefore pushes every state towards \(\ket{0}\), the maximally mixed state included: it is not unital. It is described by the amplitude-damping channel, with the Kraus operators \[ K_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{1-\gamma} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sqrt{\gamma} \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \sqrt{\gamma}\,\ket{0}\bra{1} . \tag{2.6}\] \(K_1\) is the decay itself: a qubit in \(\ket{1}\) jumps to \(\ket{0}\), with probability \(\gamma\). \(K_0\) is the case without a decay, which also shrinks the amplitude of \(\ket{1}\), since a qubit that has not decayed is more likely to have been in \(\ket{0}\) all along. Applied to a density matrix, the channel gives \[ \begin{pmatrix} \rho_{00} & \rho_{01} \\ \rho_{10} & \rho_{11} \end{pmatrix} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \rho_{00} + \gamma\rho_{11} & \sqrt{1-\gamma}\,\rho_{01} \\ \sqrt{1-\gamma}\,\rho_{10} & (1-\gamma)\,\rho_{11} \end{pmatrix} . \] In terms of the Bloch vector, \(r_z \mapsto (1-\gamma)\,r_z + \gamma\) and \(r_{x,y} \mapsto \sqrt{1-\gamma}\, r_{x,y}\): the ball shrinks, and its centre moves towards \(\ket{0}\), to \(r_z = \gamma\) (Figure 2.2 a). Non-unital noise can thus make a state purer: for \(\gamma = 1\), even the maximally mixed state becomes the pure state \(\ket{0}\).

2.2.5 Relaxation (\(T_1\)) and dephasing (\(T_2^*\)) times

Two experiments measure how fast a qubit relaxes and dephases, and define two of the times that providers quote for their devices (Section 1.5).

Definition 2.5 (Relaxation time \(T_1\)) Prepare a qubit in \(\ket{1}\) and wait for a time \(t\): the probability of still finding it in \(\ket{1}\) decays as \(e^{-t/T_1}\). \(T_1\) is called the relaxation time.

Definition 2.6 (Dephasing time \(T_2^*\)) Prepare a qubit in \(\ket{+}\) and wait for a time \(t\): its coherence, the size \(|\rho_{01}|\) of the off-diagonal entry of its density matrix, decays approximately as \(e^{-t/T_2^*}\). \(T_2^*\) is called the dephasing time. It is measured with a Ramsey experiment (Figure 2.3 a): a \(\pi/2\) pulse turns \(\ket{0}\) into \(\ket{+}\), and after the wait, a second \(\pi/2\) pulse turns the \(x\) component of the Bloch vector into the \(z\) component, which the measurement reads out.

Which channels produce these two laws? For \(T_1\), it is amplitude damping: after a time \(t\), the probability \(\rho_{11}\) of finding \(\ket{1}\) has decayed by \(1-\gamma\), so \(\gamma = 1 - e^{-t/T_1}\). Amplitude damping also shrinks the coherence, by \(\sqrt{1-\gamma} = e^{-t/2T_1}\). Even without dephasing, relaxation therefore limits the coherence: for a pure state \(\alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1}\), the coherence is \(|\alpha\beta|\), and the amplitude \(\beta\) decays at half the rate of the probability \(|\beta|^2\).

The rest of the decay is dephasing, caused by the unknown phases of Section 2.2.2. The qubit frequency is shifted by an amount that we do not know, so the phase drifts by an angle that we do not know, and after some time the information about the phase is lost. We describe this by a dephasing channel whose factor decays as \(1 - 2p = e^{-t/T_\varphi}\). This is called pure dephasing, to distinguish it from the loss of coherence caused by relaxation. Its time \(T_\varphi\) is called the pure-dephasing time. Amplitude damping and dephasing commute (Exercise 2.3), and the coherence decays with both factors, \(e^{-t/2T_1}e^{-t/T_\varphi} = e^{-t/T_2^*}\), where \[ \frac{1}{T_2^*} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\varphi} . \tag{2.7}\] Figure 2.2 b shows the combined channel.

Since \(1/T_\varphi \ge 0\), Equation 2.7 gives \(T_2^* \le 2T_1\). This bound matters in practice. The dephasing channel needs \(T_\varphi\), but \(T_\varphi\) cannot be measured on its own, because relaxation cannot be switched off: every measurement of the coherence includes the decay \(e^{-t/2T_1}\). Experiments measure \(T_1\) and \(T_2^*\), and \(T_\varphi\) follows from Equation 2.7. A noise model built from measured values therefore needs \(T_2^* \le 2T_1\). Otherwise, \(1/T_\varphi\) would be negative, and the dephasing channel would need \(p < 0\), which is not a channel. The simulators of SDKs reject or correct such values.

An idle qubit undergoes this combined channel while it waits. For a short wait \(t\), its factors are \(1 - t/T_2^*\) on the \(x\) and \(y\) axes and \(1 - t/T_1\) on the \(z\) axis, so its error is of order \(t/T_2^*\) (Section 2.6.3 makes this precise). A wait of 100 ns with \(T_2^* = 100\) µs therefore costs as much as a single-qubit gate, or more. Table 2.1 collects the single-qubit channels.

2.2.6 Echoes and \(T_2\)

The dephasing channel describes a phase that we do not know: the frequency shift \(\delta\) of Section 2.2.2, and with it the angle \(\theta = \delta t\), differs from shot to shot by an amount that we cannot predict. Physically, this is a reasonable picture when the frequency fluctuates slowly, so that \(\delta\) stays constant during each shot. But then the qubit picks up its unknown phase at a steady rate, and there is an easy way to undo it. An \(X\) pulse halfway through the wait exchanges \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\), which reverses the sign of the phase picked up so far. The second half of the wait then undoes the first, whatever the value of \(\delta\) (Exercise 2.4). This is called an echo, or Hahn echo, after Erwin Hahn, who found it with nuclear spins in 1950. It leaves the qubit flipped, so a second \(X\) is added at the end of the wait. Repeated echoes during a long wait, called dynamical decoupling, protect idle qubits from slow noise on the device itself, while the error mitigation of later chapters corrects the estimates after the run.

If the frequency also changes during the wait, the phases picked up in the two halves differ, and the echo cancels only the part they have in common. What is left comes from fluctuations on timescales shorter than the wait. The angle is then a sum of many small, independent kicks, one for each short interval, so it is Gaussian (by the central limit theorem). Its variance is the sum of the variances of the kicks, the fact behind Equation 1.9, and it therefore grows in proportion to \(t\). We write it as \(\sigma^2 = 2t/T_\varphi\), so that the coherence decays as \(e^{-\sigma^2/2} = e^{-t/T_\varphi}\). Fast noise gives the exponential decay of pure dephasing that Section 2.2.5 assumed.

An echo experiment, a Ramsey experiment with an \(X\) pulse halfway and another at the end of the wait (Figure 2.3), therefore measures a longer time than \(T_2^*\), called \(T_2\): the decay due to relaxation and fast noise only. It obeys Equation 2.7 with \(T_2\) in place of \(T_2^*\) and a longer \(T_\varphi\), so \(T_2^* \le T_2 \le 2T_1\). The star of \(T_2^*\) marks the time that includes slow noise. Dynamical decoupling is usually assumed to be available for idle qubits, so calibration data often quote \(T_2\) rather than \(T_2^*\), and the error budgets of Section 2.6.3 use \(T_2\) too. In a Ramsey experiment, slow noise makes the coherence decay faster, and the decay need not be exponential: for a frequency shift that is constant within each shot and Gaussian across shots, it is a Gaussian in \(t\) (Exercise 2.4).

Slow noise has memory, so it is the main exception to the Markovian model of Section 2.2.3. A common source is a defect in the chip that switches between two states and makes the qubit frequency jump (random telegraph noise). Many such defects give noise in which slow fluctuations dominate (1/f noise, with \(f\) the frequency of the fluctuations).

2.3 Noise on many qubits

On many qubits, the tools of Section 2.2.1 carry over with one more fact of Pauli algebra. Two Pauli strings \(P\) and \(Q\) either commute, \(PQ = QP\), or anticommute, \(PQ = -QP\); we can summarise this by defining the sign \(s_{PQ} = \pm 1\) such that \(PQ = s_{PQ}\,QP\). A Pauli channel on \(n\) qubits, \(\Lambda(\rho) = \sum_Q q_Q\, Q\rho Q\), therefore multiplies the expectation value of every Pauli string \(P\) by its own factor, \[ \langle P\rangle \mapsto f_P\langle P\rangle, \qquad f_P = \sum_Q s_{PQ}\, q_Q . \tag{2.8}\]

Noise that acts on each qubit separately, with a channel \(\Lambda_j\) on qubit \(j\), is called local: the channel on all qubits is \(\Lambda_1\otimes\cdots\otimes\Lambda_n\). It acts factor by factor on a Pauli string and leaves the identity factors unchanged, so the factor of a string is the product of the single-qubit factors at its non-identity positions.

As examples, we extend the simplest noise of Section 2.2.2, the depolarising channel, to many qubits. There are two natural ways to do this.

Definition 2.7 (Local depolarising noise) Local depolarising noise with parameter \(p\) applies the depolarising channel of Definition 2.4 to each qubit, independently.

Definition 2.8 (Global depolarising noise) The global depolarising channel on \(n\) qubits replaces the state of all qubits by the maximally mixed state with probability \(p\): \[ \Lambda(\rho) = (1-p)\,\rho + p\,\frac{I}{2^n} . \]

For \(n = 1\), both are the depolarising channel of Equation 2.5. With local depolarising noise, a Pauli string of weight \(w\), its number of non-identity positions, shrinks by \((1-p)^w\): strings of high weight lose their signal fastest.

Global depolarising behaves differently. Compare the Kraus forms first. With local depolarising noise, each qubit independently suffers \(X\), \(Y\) or \(Z\) with probability \(p/4\) each, so an error on \(w\) given qubits has a probability of order \((p/4)^w\): errors on many qubits at once are rare. The global channel is a Pauli channel too. Averaging \(Q\rho Q\) over all \(4^n\) Pauli strings \(Q\) gives \(I/2^n\). Indeed, by Equation 2.8, the factor of every non-identity string \(P\) vanishes: at a position where \(P\) is not the identity, two of the four choices of \(Q\) anticommute with it, so half of all strings do. So \[ (1-p)\,\rho + p\,\frac{I}{2^n} = \Big(1 - p + \frac{p}{4^n}\Big)\rho + \frac{p}{4^n}\sum_{Q\ne I} Q\rho Q . \] All \(4^n - 1\) non-identity errors are equally likely, and an error on all \(n\) qubits is as likely as an error on one. As a result, global depolarising shrinks every Pauli string by the same factor, \(1-p\), whatever its weight. No product of local channels does this: it would need \(f_{ZI} = f_{IZ} = f_{ZZ} = 1-p\), while a product gives \(f_{ZZ} = f_{ZI}\, f_{IZ}\).

Like its single-qubit version, global depolarising noise commutes with every unitary, here on all \(n\) qubits. If every gate \(i\) of a circuit is followed by global depolarising noise with parameter \(p_i\), all of it can be moved to the end of the circuit. Every expectation value then shrinks by the same factor: \[ \langle P\rangle \mapsto f\langle P\rangle, \qquad f = \prod_i (1-p_i) . \tag{2.9}\] A single number describes the noise of the whole circuit. This makes global depolarising noise much easier to model than local noise, and to correct for (Section 2.7.2).

Local noise does not commute with entangling gates. Conjugation by CZ turns an \(X\) on the second qubit into \(Z\otimes X\) (Section 1.3.4), so an \(X\) error before a CZ is a two-qubit error after it. Errors therefore spread through a circuit: each later two-qubit gate can carry an error to one more qubit, and the qubits that it can reach form a cone, called its light cone (Figure 2.4). For deep circuits of random gates, global depolarising noise is then a good approximation of local noise. These circuits are called scrambling: the light cone of every error soon covers all the qubits, so that any error, wherever it happens, leaves the output close to random. Global depolarising noise is therefore a very useful model for estimates (Section 2.6 and Section 2.7), although real noise is mostly local.

Correlated errors affect several qubits together. An example is crosstalk (Section 1.2.3), including the coupling between neighbouring superconducting qubits that is never fully off and acts as a small, unwanted \(R_{ZZ}\) rotation (Equation 1.3). Some correlated errors affect a whole chip. When a cosmic ray, or the radioactive decay of a trace impurity, deposits energy in a superconducting chip, the energy spreads as vibrations of the crystal (phonons), and for tens of milliseconds all qubits on the chip relax much faster than usual. In the experiment of McEwen et al. (2022), this happened about once every ten seconds. Such bursts break the independence of errors that the estimates of Section 2.6 assume, and they matter most for error correction.

2.4 Noisy gates

In Section 2.2.3, we modelled the noisy gates of a device as ideal gates, each followed by a channel. But which channel is the right one? Part of it is the relaxation and dephasing during the gate. The rest comes from the control: the pulses that implement a gate are never exactly right.

A coherent error (Section 1.5) is a fixed, wrong unitary. A pulse that is slightly too strong, for example, implements \(R_X(\theta+\eta) = R_X(\eta)R_X(\theta)\) instead of \(R_X(\theta)\): the channel after the gate is the unitary \(R_X(\eta)\). Biased angles in parametrised gates return with variational algorithms. An incoherent error is random: the error unitary differs from shot to shot, and averages to a channel, as the random \(R_Z\) did in Section 2.2.2.

The difference matters when a gate is repeated. Apply an \(X\) gate \(n\) times to a qubit that starts in \(\ket{0}\), each time over-rotated about the \(x\) axis by a small angle \(\eta_k\). The result is \(X^n\) followed by \(R_X(\sum_k\eta_k)\). A total error angle \(\theta\) gives the wrong outcome with probability \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\). We use this error probability as the measure of error until Section 2.6. If the error is the same every time, \(\eta_k = \eta\), the angles add up like the slow noise of Section 2.2.6, and the error probability is \(\sin^2(n\eta/2) \approx n^2\eta^2/4\). If the errors are random and independent, with mean zero and variance \(\sigma^2\), they add up like the fast noise there: the variance of the total angle is \(n\sigma^2\), and the average error probability is about \(n\sigma^2/4\). For the same error per gate, \(\eta = \sigma\), the coherent error after \(n\) gates is \(n\) times larger (Figure 2.5).

Three consequences matter later:

  1. The error budgets of Section 2.6 assume incoherent errors, so they underestimate coherent ones.
  2. Circuits that repeat identical layers, such as those for time evolution, are the worst case.
  3. Coherent errors can be made incoherent on purpose: random Pauli gates before and after a gate such as CZ, chosen so that they cancel in the ideal circuit, average its error into a Pauli channel. This is called twirling. Each random Pauli gate can be merged with the single-qubit gate next to it, which costs a single pulse anyway (Section 1.3.5), so twirling needs no extra pulses. Exercise 2.5 (b) shows the simplest case.

Leakage falls outside this description: it takes a qubit out of its two levels, for example into the third level of a transmon (Figure 1.2), so that channels on \(2\times 2\) density matrices cannot describe it.

2.5 Readout errors

A measurement can report the wrong bit (Table 1.4). We describe this with the confusion matrix \(M\), whose entry \(M_{ij} = p(\text{read } i \mid \text{state } j)\) is the probability of reading \(i\) when the qubit is in \(\ket{j}\). On one qubit, \[ M = \begin{pmatrix} 1-e_0 & e_1 \\ e_0 & 1-e_1 \end{pmatrix}, \] where \(e_0 = p(1\mid 0)\) and \(e_1 = p(0\mid 1)\) are the two error rates. If we call reading 1 a positive, \(e_0\) is the rate of false positives and \(e_1\) the rate of false negatives. \(M\) maps the probabilities of the true outcomes to those of the recorded ones, so it is a channel on classical probabilities (Walter and Ozols 2025, sec. 4.3).

If the two error rates are equal, \(e_0 = e_1 = e\), the readout acts as a perfect measurement after a bit flip: the Pauli channel that applies \(X\) with probability \(e\). By Equation 2.4, \(\langle Z\rangle\) then shrinks by \(1-2e\), as in Exercise 1.9. Usually, \(e_1 > e_0\). A readout on a superconducting qubit takes about a microsecond (Table 1.5), and a qubit in \(\ket{1}\) can relax during it, with a probability of order 1% for \(T_1 = 100\) µs. With \(p_0 = (1+\langle Z\rangle)/2\) and \(p_1 = (1-\langle Z\rangle)/2\), the recorded expectation value is then \[ \langle Z\rangle_{\text{read}} = (1 - e_0 - e_1)\,\langle Z\rangle + (e_1 - e_0) , \tag{2.10}\] a shrinking factor and an offset, as for amplitude damping.

Preparation can fail too. A qubit that is thermally excited (Exercise 1.1) starts in \(\ket{1}\) instead of \(\ket{0}\), and a measurement right after the preparation cannot tell this apart from a readout error (Exercise 2.6). Errors in state preparation and measurement are therefore characterised together, as SPAM errors.

On \(n\) qubits, the confusion matrix is \(2^n\times 2^n\): its entry \(M_{xy}\) is the probability of reading the bit string \(x\) when the qubits are in the basis state \(\ket{y}\). For independent readouts, it is the tensor product of single-qubit matrices. If, in addition, each qubit’s two error rates are equal, every qubit of a Pauli string shrinks its expectation value by its own factor \(1 - 2e_j\) (Exercise 1.9). Otherwise, the offsets of Equation 2.10 also mix in the expectation values of shorter strings. Correcting estimates for known readout errors is the simplest form of error mitigation, which we meet later in the course.

2.6 Fidelity and error budgets

Providers summarise the quality of each operation by a fidelity (Section 1.5). In this section, we define fidelities, and we use the error rates that providers quote for a quick estimate of how much signal a circuit keeps. We work out one example, the same circuit on two platforms.

2.6.1 Fidelity of states and gates

Definition 2.9 (Fidelity) The state fidelity of a state \(\rho\) with a pure target state \(\ket{\psi}\) is \(F = \bra{\psi}\rho\ket{\psi}\). The average gate fidelity \(F_{\text{avg}}\) of a noisy gate is the state fidelity of its output with the ideal output \(U\ket{\psi}\), averaged over all pure input states \(\ket{\psi}\). The error per gate is \(r = 1 - F_{\text{avg}}\).

The state fidelity is the probability that \(\rho\) passes a measurement that asks “is the state \(\ket{\psi}\)?”. Some texts, including Nielsen and Chuang (2010) and the Quantum Information Theory notes (Walter and Ozols 2025, sec. 4.2), call \(\sqrt{\bra{\psi}\rho\ket{\psi}}\) the fidelity instead. We use the squared version, as Qiskit does.

For example, after depolarising noise with parameter \(p\), every pure state has the fidelity \(1 - p/2\) with the ideal one, so \(r = p/2\).

Providers usually quote the error per gate \(r\), measured with randomised benchmarking. An error budget needs instead the probability that an operation goes wrong. For a Pauli channel, this is the Pauli error \(\varepsilon = 1 - q_I\), the probability of an error other than the identity. It is slightly larger than \(r\). On \(d\) levels (\(d = 2^n\) for \(n\) qubits), \[ \varepsilon = \frac{d+1}{d}\, r , \tag{2.11}\] so \(\varepsilon = 3r/2\) for a single-qubit gate and \(5r/4\) for a two-qubit gate. Research papers often quote Pauli errors instead of \(r\), so a budget must check which of the two it uses. A derivation of Equation 2.11 can be found in Nielsen (2002).

2.6.2 Circuit fidelity and the signal left

A circuit has many gates, each with a small error. Before we run it, we would like to know how much of the ideal result survives: by how much a measured expectation value shrinks, and whether what is left can be seen above the shot noise. Computing this exactly means simulating the noisy circuit, with a detailed noise model. Instead, we estimate it from the error rates that providers quote, with a few approximations.

Suppose every gate and idle step \(i\) of a circuit suffers a Pauli error with probability \(\varepsilon_i\), independently of the others. The probability that a shot runs without any error is then \[ f = \prod_i (1-\varepsilon_i) \approx e^{-\sum_i\varepsilon_i} , \tag{2.12}\] for small \(\varepsilon_i\), as in Section 1.1 and Exercise 1.8. Virtual \(Z\) gates add nothing to the sum (Section 1.3.5). In a scrambling circuit (Section 2.3), any error leaves the output close to random, so the noise acts as global depolarising noise. Every expectation value then shrinks by the same factor \(f\), as in Equation 2.9 with \(p_i \approx \varepsilon_i\). So \(f\), the probability of a shot without an error, is also the fraction of the signal that is left, and approximately the fidelity of the output state with the ideal one.

Readout errors act after the last gate and do not spread. With equal error rates \(e\), they shrink a Pauli string of weight \(w\) by \((1-2e)^w \approx e^{-2ew}\) (Exercise 1.9). Each qubit of the string therefore adds \(2e\) to the sum in Equation 2.12, and the other qubits add nothing.

These estimates assume independent, incoherent errors and a circuit that spreads them. They are rough, but they are what we use to judge whether a result is plausible. With \(N\) shots, the standard error is about \(1/\sqrt{N}\) (Equation 1.9). A measured value \(f\langle P\rangle\) can therefore be told apart from zero only if \(f|\langle P\rangle|\) is several times larger than \(1/\sqrt{N}\).

2.6.3 Example: error budgets on two platforms

As an example, we estimate how much signal one circuit keeps on a superconducting and on a trapped-ion device. Take the brick-wall circuit of Exercise 1.8, on a line of 10 qubits with 8 layers, and estimate \(\langle Z\otimes Z\rangle\) on two neighbouring qubits. Each layer applies an \(R_X\) pulse to every qubit, its \(R_Z\) being virtual, and CZs between neighbours: 80 pulses and 36 CZs. We take values that are typical of current devices (Section 1.5 and Table 1.5):

  • superconducting: Pauli errors of 0.03% per pulse (40 ns), 0.5% per CZ (80 ns) and 1% per readout, \(T_1 = 150\) µs and \(T_2 = 100\) µs;
  • trapped ions: 0.01% per pulse (20 µs), 0.2% per two-qubit gate (300 µs) and 0.3% per readout, \(T_2 = 1\) s and a much longer \(T_1\), with all gates run one after another.

Idle qubits contribute too. Over a short wait \(t\), the error probability of an idle qubit is about \(t/(2T_2) + t/(4T_1)\), from the factors of Section 2.2.5. It uses \(T_2\) in place of \(T_2^*\), since dynamical decoupling protects the idle qubits (Section 2.2.6). On the superconducting device, only the two qubits at the ends of the line wait, during the CZs of every other layer. On the ion trap, each qubit waits for about 10 ms of the 12 ms that the gates take. Table 2.2 collects the contributions.

On the superconducting device, the two-qubit gates dominate, followed by the readout. Idling hardly matters, since all qubits are busy in most layers. On the ion trap, every error rate is lower, but the gates take long and run one after another. Each qubit therefore waits for most of the shot, and idling costs almost as much as the two-qubit gates. In general, readout errors dominate wide, shallow circuits and observables of high weight, while gate errors dominate deep circuits.

The same specifications define a noise model for a simulator: after each gate, a depolarising channel with the relaxation and dephasing of its duration, together matching the quoted error; the same relaxation and dephasing on idle qubits; and a confusion matrix per qubit. Such a model is only as good as the last calibration (Section 1.5).

2.7 Bias and shot noise

2.7.1 Mean squared error

In Section 1.4.3, the sample mean \(\bar s\) of \(N\) shots was an unbiased estimator of \(\langle P\rangle\). With noise, each shot comes from the noisy state, and \(\bar s\) averages to \(f\langle P\rangle\) instead. Its error now has two parts. The bias is the difference between its average and the value we want, \(b = \mathbb{E}[\bar s] - \langle P\rangle = (f-1)\langle P\rangle\). Its variance is \((1 - f^2\langle P\rangle^2)/N\), the square of Equation 1.9 with \(f\langle P\rangle\) in place of \(\langle P\rangle\). The two add up in the mean squared error: \[ \mathbb{E}\big[(\bar s - \langle P\rangle)^2\big] = b^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\bar s) = (1-f)^2\langle P\rangle^2 + \frac{1 - f^2\langle P\rangle^2}{N} . \tag{2.13}\] To see the first equality, write \(\bar s - \langle P\rangle = (\bar s - \mathbb{E}[\bar s]) + b\) and expand the square: the cross term averages to zero. More shots shrink only the variance, while the bias stays.

The two terms are equal at \[ N^* = \frac{1 - f^2\langle P\rangle^2}{(1-f)^2\langle P\rangle^2} . \] Beyond \(N^*\) shots, the bias dominates the error, and more shots hardly help: they estimate the wrong value more precisely. For the superconducting example of Table 2.2, with \(f = 0.78\) and \(\langle P\rangle = 0.5\), \(N^* \approx 70\) shots. A larger circuit has a smaller \(f\), and an even smaller \(N^*\).

2.7.2 Correcting a known shrinking factor

If the shrinking factor \(f\) is known, the bias can be removed: \(\bar s/f\) averages to \(\langle P\rangle\). The price is in the variance. Dividing by \(f\) multiplies the standard error by \(1/f\), so the same precision as without noise takes \(1/f^2 = e^{2\sum_i\varepsilon_i}\) times as many shots. The cost grows exponentially with the total error of the circuit. For the superconducting example, this is a factor of 1.6. For a circuit ten times larger, with \(f \approx 0.08\), it is a factor of about 140. This is the simplest method of error mitigation, applied to readout errors in Exercise 1.9 (d).

Knowing \(f\) is the hard part. For global depolarising noise, every expectation value shrinks by the same factor, and the error rates of the device give it (Equation 2.12). Readout errors, which act only at the end of the circuit, are another simple case. For generic circuits and noise models, however, each expectation value is changed in its own way, and predicting it means simulating the noisy circuit. A cost in shots of the same kind returns with the other mitigation methods later in the course.

Two questions follow from here. The observables of real applications, such as the energy of a physical system, are sums of many Pauli strings, each estimated with its own shots and its own noise. And the bias need not be accepted: error-mitigation methods estimate and remove it, at a price in shots.
